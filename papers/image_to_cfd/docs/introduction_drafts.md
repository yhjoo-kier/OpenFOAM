## Version A: Problem-Driven (실내 CFD 수요 → 형상 병목 → VLM 해결책)

### 1. Introduction

#### 1.1 Motivation

Indoor airflow simulation has become an essential tool for assessing ventilation effectiveness, thermal comfort, and contaminant transport in enclosed spaces. The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the critical role of computational fluid dynamics (CFD) in evaluating how airborne pathogens disperse within rooms, restaurants, and offices, driving an unprecedented surge in indoor CFD studies [Vuorinen 2020 aerosol; Liu 2021 restaurant COVID]. Beyond pandemic-related applications, CFD-based airflow analysis is routinely employed in building energy performance assessment [Brozovsky 2022 microclimate energy], heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC) system design [Ren 2019 linear ventilation], and indoor air quality evaluation [Liu 2019 PV review]. In industrial settings, CFD is increasingly used to diagnose thermal inefficiencies in manufacturing facilities, where airflow patterns directly affect process energy consumption and worker comfort [Gourlis 2017 industrial BIM energy; Zhao 2021 reheating furnace].

Despite its analytical power, CFD simulation of indoor environments remains a specialist activity that requires substantial manual effort. The standard workflow begins with the construction of a three-dimensional geometric model of the indoor space, typically using computer-aided design (CAD) software or building information modelling (BIM) tools. This geometry preparation step — which includes defining room boundaries, locating internal obstacles such as furniture or equipment, and specifying ventilation openings — is widely recognised as the most time-consuming phase of the CFD workflow [Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD]. For a moderately complex indoor space, an experienced engineer may spend several hours to days creating a simulation-ready model, a cost that is disproportionate to the minutes or hours required for the subsequent meshing and solving steps. This bottleneck limits the broader adoption of CFD-based airflow analysis, particularly in rapid screening applications such as energy audits of industrial facilities, post-occupancy ventilation assessments, and preliminary design evaluations where approximate flow patterns — rather than high-fidelity local predictions — would be sufficient.

Several approaches have been explored to reduce the geometry preparation burden. Building information modelling (BIM) platforms can export geometric data to CFD-compatible formats, but this pathway requires that a BIM model already exists, which is often not the case for existing buildings or industrial facilities [Utkucu 2020 BIM interoperability; Weerasuriya 2019 BIM ventilation]. Scan-to-BIM methods based on terrestrial laser scanning or photogrammetry can reconstruct detailed 3D models from point clouds, but these methods require specialised equipment, multi-view data acquisition, and substantial post-processing to produce watertight meshable surfaces [Rocha 2020 scan-to-BIM; Wang 2019 scan-to-BIM]. Parameterised geometry generators can produce families of simulation-ready models from a small number of design parameters, but they are limited to predefined topologies and cannot handle arbitrary room layouts. In all cases, the input to the CFD pipeline is a structured 3D representation; the gap between a raw visual observation — such as a photograph or an architectural drawing — and a simulation-ready geometry has remained a manual step.

The recent emergence of vision-language models (VLMs) offers a fundamentally different approach to this problem. Models such as GPT-4 [OpenAI 2023 GPT-4] and Gemini [Google 2024 Gemini] have demonstrated strong spatial reasoning capabilities, including the ability to interpret architectural drawings, estimate room dimensions from photographs, and enumerate objects from overhead images [Chen 2024 SpatialVLM; Hong 2023 3D-LLM]. These capabilities suggest that a VLM could serve as a geometric abstraction engine: given a single 2D image of an indoor space, the VLM extracts a structured 3D scene description comprising room boundaries, obstacle bounding boxes, and ventilation openings, which can then be automatically converted into a computational mesh and boundary conditions for a CFD solver. Unlike geometric reconstruction methods that aim for photorealistic fidelity, this abstraction approach produces a simplified but simulation-compatible representation — precisely the level of detail required for engineering screening analysis.

This paper presents an automated framework that implements this concept and evaluates it through a controlled benchmark. A single 2D image is processed by a VLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash) to produce a structured 3D scene description, which is then automatically meshed and solved using OpenFOAM [Weller 1998 OpenFOAM]. A rule-based benchmark dataset of 20 indoor geometries spanning four complexity levels and five input view types (100 evaluation cases in total) is constructed with independently computed reference CFD solutions, ensuring that no VLM output contaminates the ground truth. The framework is evaluated using structural similarity and reference CFD agreement metrics that quantify both geometric fidelity and downstream flow-field accuracy.

#### 1.2 Related work

**Image-based 3D reconstruction.** Research at the intersection of computer vision and 3D modelling has produced powerful reconstruction methods. Neural radiance fields (NeRF) [Mildenhall 2020 NeRF] and their language-augmented variants [Kerr 2023 LERF] can synthesise photorealistic novel views from multi-view images, but their implicit volumetric representations do not yield the watertight, topologically clean surface meshes required by finite-volume CFD solvers. Multi-view stereo methods produce explicit point clouds or meshes, but these typically contain noise, holes, and non-manifold edges that prevent direct use in meshing pipelines. Floor-plan recognition and parsing methods can extract room layouts from architectural drawings, but they produce 2D polygonal outputs without the 3D volumetric information (room height, obstacle vertical extent, opening elevation) needed for CFD domain definition. In all cases, a substantial post-processing effort remains between the reconstruction output and a CFD-ready geometry.

**CFD automation and surrogate models.** On the simulation side, efforts to automate the CFD workflow have focused on parameterised geometry generators, template-based meshing, and automated solver configuration [Hou 2022 BIM CFD prediction]. These approaches assume that a structured geometric input (CAD model, BIM export, or parameter vector) already exists, and they automate the downstream steps of meshing, boundary condition assignment, and solver execution. A separate line of work has explored end-to-end surrogate models that predict flow fields directly from geometric parameters or images using convolutional neural networks [Guo 2016 CNN flow prediction] or generative adversarial networks [Kastner 2023 GAN urban wind]. While these approaches bypass the meshing step entirely, they are constrained to the training distribution and cannot generalise to novel geometries without retraining. The present work differs in that it retains the full-fidelity CFD solver and uses the VLM solely for the geometric abstraction step, thereby preserving the physical rigour of the simulation while automating the geometry preparation.

**Vision-language models for spatial understanding.** VLMs have recently demonstrated spatial reasoning capabilities that extend beyond image captioning. SpatialVLM [Chen 2024 SpatialVLM] showed that VLMs can estimate metric distances and spatial relationships between objects when trained on spatially grounded data. 3D-LLM [Hong 2023 3D-LLM] injected 3D scene representations into large language models, enabling them to answer questions about spatial layouts. These results suggest that VLMs possess sufficient spatial perception to extract the coarse geometric information (room dimensions, obstacle placement, opening locations) needed for engineering-level CFD abstraction. However, no prior work has systematically evaluated the downstream simulation accuracy of VLM-generated scene descriptions, nor has a controlled benchmark been established to isolate the effect of input view type from geometric complexity.

#### 1.3 Contributions

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

1. **Framework**: An automated pipeline that converts a single 2D image of an indoor space into a steady-state CFD solution, using a VLM as the geometric abstraction engine and OpenFOAM as the solver.
2. **Benchmark dataset**: A rule-based dataset of 20 indoor geometries across four complexity levels, rendered into five view types (100 cases), with independently computed reference CFD solutions free from VLM data leakage.
3. **Quantitative evaluation**: A systematic analysis of framework performance across view types and complexity categories, using structural and reference CFD agreement metrics with component-level decomposition.
4. **Failure-mode analysis**: Identification and characterisation of three systematic failure modes — composite-room collapse, obstacle hallucination, and the structure-versus-fidelity gap — that delineate the current capabilities and limitations of VLM-based geometric abstraction for CFD.

## Version B: Technology Convergence (VLM 발전 + CFD 자동화 → 교차점)

### 1. Introduction

#### 1.1 Motivation

Two largely independent technological trajectories are now converging in a way that opens a previously inaccessible capability. On one side, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has matured into the standard tool for indoor airflow analysis, with applications spanning ventilation design [Kosutova 2019 cross-ventilation], thermal comfort assessment [Chen 2021 thermal comfort CFD], contaminant dispersion modelling [Vuorinen 2020 aerosol], and building energy simulation [Brozovsky 2022 microclimate energy]. The physics-based rigour of CFD makes it irreplaceable for applications where local flow details — recirculation zones, jet trajectories, temperature stratification — determine the engineering outcome. On the other side, vision-language models (VLMs) have undergone a rapid capability expansion, progressing from image captioning and visual question answering to spatial reasoning tasks that require metric distance estimation, object localisation in 3D, and scene layout understanding [Chen 2024 SpatialVLM; Hong 2023 3D-LLM].

The intersection of these two trajectories raises a concrete question: can the spatial perception capabilities of a VLM extract, from a single 2D image, a 3D scene description that is sufficiently accurate to produce a meaningful CFD solution? This question has not been addressed in the literature, because the two communities — computer vision and computational engineering — have operated with different output requirements. Vision researchers evaluate 3D understanding in terms of depth accuracy, object detection metrics, or scene graph completeness, none of which directly predict whether a reconstructed geometry will produce a convergent and physically plausible CFD solution. Conversely, CFD practitioners have not considered VLMs as a source of geometric input, because the standard workflow assumes a pre-existing CAD or BIM model.

The practical motivation for connecting these trajectories is the geometry preparation bottleneck in indoor CFD. Constructing a 3D model of an existing indoor space — defining room boundaries, placing obstacles, specifying inlet and outlet locations — is the most labour-intensive step in the simulation workflow [Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD]. This cost is justifiable for high-stakes applications (operating-room ventilation, cleanroom design) where simulation accuracy must be maximised. But for screening applications — rapid ventilation assessment during energy audits, preliminary airflow evaluation in facility planning, or post-occupancy comfort diagnostics — the geometry preparation cost is disproportionate to the required accuracy. In such applications, an approximate flow field derived from a photograph or floor plan would provide actionable information at a fraction of the current cost.

This paper presents a framework that operationalises this convergence. A single 2D image of an indoor space is processed by a VLM (Gemini 2.0 Flash) [Google 2024 Gemini], which produces a structured JSON description of the room geometry, internal obstacles, and ventilation openings. This description is automatically converted into a computational mesh using Gmsh [Geuzaine 2009 Gmsh] and solved to steady state using OpenFOAM's simpleFoam solver with the SIMPLE pressure-velocity coupling algorithm [Patankar 1980 numerical heat transfer]. The framework is evaluated on a controlled benchmark of 100 cases (20 geometries × 5 view types), with reference CFD solutions computed from ground-truth geometries to enable rigorous quantitative assessment.

#### 1.2 Related work

**Indoor CFD and the geometry bottleneck.** CFD simulation of indoor environments has been extensively applied to ventilation effectiveness [Liu 2019 PV review], airborne infection risk [Liu 2021 restaurant COVID; Srivastava 2021 ventilation COVID], and building energy performance [Ren 2019 linear ventilation]. Reviews of CFD practice in the built environment consistently identify geometry preparation as a major barrier to wider adoption [Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD]. BIM-to-CFD workflows can partially automate this step when a digital building model exists [Utkucu 2020 BIM interoperability; Weerasuriya 2019 BIM ventilation; Hou 2022 BIM CFD prediction], but BIM models are unavailable for many existing structures, particularly older buildings and industrial facilities [Gourlis 2017 industrial BIM energy]. Scan-to-BIM approaches reconstruct 3D models from laser scans or photogrammetry [Rocha 2020 scan-to-BIM], but require multi-view data acquisition and extensive post-processing to produce simulation-ready surfaces.

**Data-driven alternatives to CFD.** To circumvent the full CFD workflow, several studies have explored surrogate models that predict flow fields from geometric parameters or images. Guo et al. [Guo 2016 CNN flow prediction] demonstrated that convolutional neural networks can approximate steady-state flow fields orders of magnitude faster than traditional solvers. More recent work has applied generative adversarial networks to urban wind flow prediction [Kastner 2023 GAN urban wind] and deep learning to indoor airflow estimation [Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD]. While these approaches eliminate the meshing and solving steps entirely, they are inherently limited to the training distribution and cannot generalise to geometries outside their training set without retraining. The present work retains the full-fidelity CFD solver, ensuring physical consistency and generalisability, and uses the VLM only for the geometric abstraction step.

**VLM spatial reasoning.** Vision-language models have progressed beyond image-level understanding to fine-grained spatial reasoning. SpatialVLM [Chen 2024 SpatialVLM] demonstrated metric spatial understanding by training on large-scale spatial question-answering data. 3D-LLM [Hong 2023 3D-LLM] integrated 3D point-cloud features into language models to enable spatial dialogue. Language-embedded radiance fields [Kerr 2023 LERF] connected semantic language queries with 3D scene representations. These advances establish that VLMs can extract spatially grounded information from visual inputs, but no study has evaluated whether this information is sufficient in accuracy and completeness to serve as input to a physics-based simulation.

**Image-based 3D reconstruction.** Classical and neural methods for 3D reconstruction — including neural radiance fields [Mildenhall 2020 NeRF], multi-view stereo, and floor-plan parsing — produce representations optimised for visual fidelity rather than simulation compatibility. NeRF and its variants produce implicit volumetric fields that cannot be directly meshed. Floor-plan parsers extract 2D room layouts but discard the vertical dimension. The VLM-based approach proposed here differs fundamentally: it does not reconstruct the scene geometry but abstracts it into a simulation-compatible schema (axis-aligned blocks, bounding boxes, wall-anchored openings), trading visual fidelity for meshability.

#### 1.3 Contributions

This paper makes four contributions:

1. An end-to-end framework that converts a single 2D image into a steady-state indoor CFD solution via VLM-based geometric abstraction, bridging the gap between visual perception and physics-based simulation.
2. A controlled benchmark dataset of 100 evaluation cases (20 geometries × 5 view types) with ground-truth reference CFD solutions, designed to isolate the effects of input view type and geometric complexity.
3. A dual-metric evaluation protocol comprising structural similarity (geometric fidelity) and reference CFD agreement (flow-field accuracy), with component-level decomposition enabling detailed failure-mode analysis.
4. Systematic identification of three failure modes that define the current performance envelope of VLM-based geometric abstraction for CFD applications.

## Version C: Gap-Driven (image-to-3D vs CFD 요구사항 사이의 간극)

### 1. Introduction

#### 1.1 Motivation

Converting a two-dimensional image into a three-dimensional representation has been a central objective of computer vision research for decades. Recent advances in neural implicit representations — particularly neural radiance fields (NeRF) [Mildenhall 2020 NeRF] and their extensions [Kerr 2023 LERF] — have achieved photorealistic quality in novel view synthesis from multi-view inputs. Single-image 3D reconstruction methods, floor-plan recognition systems, and depth estimation networks have further expanded the range of visual inputs from which 3D information can be extracted. However, the outputs of these methods — implicit volumetric fields, noisy point clouds, or 2D polygonal layouts — are fundamentally incompatible with the requirements of finite-volume CFD solvers, which demand watertight, topologically clean surface meshes with well-defined boundary conditions.

Simultaneously, CFD simulation of indoor environments has become an indispensable engineering tool. Applications range from ventilation system design [Kosutova 2019 cross-ventilation] and indoor air quality assessment [Liu 2019 PV review] to airborne pathogen transport modelling [Vuorinen 2020 aerosol; Liu 2021 restaurant COVID] and building energy analysis [Brozovsky 2022 microclimate energy]. The demand for indoor CFD has intensified with growing awareness of indoor environmental quality and its impact on occupant health, productivity, and energy consumption. Yet the standard CFD workflow begins with a manually constructed 3D geometric model — a step that requires CAD expertise, detailed knowledge of the physical space, and substantial engineering time [Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD].

A gap therefore persists between two mature capabilities: the ability to extract 3D information from images, and the ability to simulate airflow in 3D geometries. On one side, image-to-3D methods produce visually rich but simulation-incompatible outputs. On the other side, CFD automation methods [Hou 2022 BIM CFD prediction; Weerasuriya 2019 BIM ventilation] produce high-fidelity simulations but require structured geometric inputs (CAD models, BIM exports, or design parameters) that are unavailable when the starting point is a raw image. The manual geometry preparation step that bridges this gap has resisted automation because it requires both visual interpretation (identifying what objects are present and where) and engineering judgement (deciding which geometric features are relevant for simulation and how to represent them).

This paper proposes that vision-language models (VLMs) can bridge this gap through geometric *abstraction* rather than geometric *reconstruction*. Unlike reconstruction methods that aim to recover the full geometric detail of a scene, the proposed approach uses a VLM to extract only the information needed for CFD simulation: room envelope dimensions, obstacle bounding boxes, and ventilation opening locations and types. This abstraction is expressed as a structured JSON schema that can be directly converted into a computational mesh. The key insight is that indoor CFD at the screening level does not require centimetre-accurate surface geometry; it requires topologically correct room boundaries, approximately placed obstacles, and correctly identified ventilation openings — precisely the type of coarse spatial information that VLMs have been shown to extract from images [Chen 2024 SpatialVLM; Hong 2023 3D-LLM].

#### 1.2 Related work

**Image-to-3D reconstruction for built environments.** Neural radiance fields [Mildenhall 2020 NeRF] represent scenes as continuous volumetric functions learned from multi-view images, achieving state-of-the-art novel view synthesis. However, extracting watertight surface meshes from NeRF representations remains an open challenge, and the multi-view requirement is impractical for rapid single-image applications. Floor-plan recognition methods can parse architectural drawings into vectorised room layouts, but they operate in 2D and do not recover the vertical dimension needed for CFD domain definition. Scan-to-BIM pipelines [Rocha 2020 scan-to-BIM; Wang 2019 scan-to-BIM] produce detailed 3D models from point clouds, but they require specialised scanning equipment and significant manual intervention to achieve simulation-ready quality. The common limitation is that these methods prioritise geometric fidelity over simulation compatibility.

**CFD automation and the structured-input assumption.** Efforts to automate the indoor CFD workflow have produced BIM-to-CFD pipelines [Utkucu 2020 BIM interoperability; Weerasuriya 2019 BIM ventilation], parameterised geometry generators, and automated meshing tools [Geuzaine 2009 Gmsh]. These approaches successfully automate the downstream steps (meshing, solver configuration, post-processing) but share a common assumption: that a structured 3D geometric input already exists. When this assumption is violated — as it typically is for existing buildings without BIM models, for industrial facilities with ad-hoc layouts, or for rapid field assessments — the user is left with the manual geometry preparation bottleneck.

**Surrogate and data-driven flow prediction.** An alternative strategy avoids the meshing step entirely by training neural networks to predict flow fields from geometric inputs. Guo et al. [Guo 2016 CNN flow prediction] pioneered this approach with convolutional neural networks for steady flow approximation. Subsequent work has extended the concept to urban wind prediction [Kastner 2023 GAN urban wind] and building ventilation [Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD]. These surrogate models offer dramatic speed improvements but sacrifice physical generalisability: they cannot predict flows in geometries outside their training distribution without retraining, and they do not provide the conservation guarantees of a finite-volume solver.

**VLM spatial perception.** The emergence of VLMs with spatial reasoning capabilities [Chen 2024 SpatialVLM; Hong 2023 3D-LLM] provides a new pathway. Rather than reconstructing geometry or predicting flow fields directly, a VLM can serve as a semantic interpreter that converts unstructured visual input into structured geometric descriptions. This approach decouples the visual interpretation problem (handled by the VLM) from the simulation problem (handled by the CFD solver), preserving the physical rigour of the simulation while automating the interpretation step. The present work is the first to implement and systematically evaluate this approach for indoor CFD applications.

#### 1.3 Contributions

The contributions of this work are:

1. A framework that bridges the gap between image-based scene understanding and physics-based simulation by using VLM geometric abstraction (rather than reconstruction) as the interface between a single 2D image and a steady-state CFD solution.
2. A controlled benchmark dataset with 100 evaluation cases and independent reference CFD solutions, designed to quantify the downstream simulation impact of VLM-based geometric abstraction across multiple view types and complexity levels.
3. A decomposed evaluation methodology that separately measures geometric fidelity (structural score) and flow-field accuracy (reference CFD agreement score), revealing the non-trivial relationship between geometric accuracy and simulation accuracy.
4. A systematic failure-mode taxonomy (composite-room collapse, obstacle hallucination, structure-versus-fidelity gap) that characterises the performance envelope and identifies the most impactful sources of error.

## Version D: Industrial Energy Efficiency (산업 에너지 진단 → CFD 보급 장벽 → 자동화)

### 1. Introduction

#### 1.1 Motivation

Energy efficiency improvement in industrial manufacturing processes is a global priority. Manufacturing facilities account for a substantial fraction of total energy consumption in industrialised economies, and governmental and corporate carbon-reduction targets are driving systematic energy audits across sectors [Gourlis 2017 industrial BIM energy; Zhao 2021 reheating furnace]. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that indoor airflow patterns within factories, warehouses, and processing halls have a direct impact on HVAC energy consumption, process thermal management, and worker thermal comfort. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation is the most powerful diagnostic tool available for analysing these airflow patterns, as it can reveal recirculation zones, thermal stratification, short-circuiting of supply air, and other phenomena that are invisible to point measurements and difficult to infer from energy consumption data alone [Vuorinen 2020 aerosol; Liu 2019 PV review].

Despite its diagnostic value, CFD-based airflow analysis is rarely included in standard energy audit workflows for industrial facilities. The primary barrier is not the simulation itself — modern solvers such as OpenFOAM [Weller 1998 OpenFOAM] can handle typical indoor geometries in minutes to hours — but the upstream geometry preparation step. An energy auditor visiting a factory floor typically has access to architectural drawings (often outdated or incomplete), photographs taken during the site visit, and perhaps a rough sketch of the space. Converting this visual and documentary information into a three-dimensional geometric model suitable for CFD simulation requires CAD expertise, detailed spatial measurements, and several hours to days of modelling work — resources that are rarely available within the time and budget constraints of a standard energy audit [Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD]. This geometry bottleneck effectively excludes CFD from the energy diagnostician's toolkit, limiting airflow assessment to qualitative observation or costly ad-hoc studies commissioned from specialist consultants.

Existing approaches to reducing this bottleneck have not fully addressed the problem. BIM-to-CFD workflows [Utkucu 2020 BIM interoperability; Weerasuriya 2019 BIM ventilation; Hou 2022 BIM CFD prediction] can streamline the process when a digital building model exists, but BIM adoption in the industrial sector remains limited, particularly for older facilities that were constructed before digital modelling became standard practice [Gourlis 2017 industrial BIM energy]. Scan-to-BIM methods [Rocha 2020 scan-to-BIM] require specialised equipment and multi-view data collection that are impractical during a time-constrained site visit. Surrogate models based on deep learning [Guo 2016 CNN flow prediction; Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD; Kastner 2023 GAN urban wind] can predict flow fields rapidly, but they are limited to pre-trained geometry families and cannot handle the diverse and often irregular layouts encountered in industrial environments.

The ideal capability for an energy diagnostician would be to photograph a facility space — or use an existing floor plan — and obtain a CFD-quality airflow assessment within minutes, with no manual geometry modelling required. The recent emergence of vision-language models (VLMs) with spatial reasoning capabilities [OpenAI 2023 GPT-4; Google 2024 Gemini; Chen 2024 SpatialVLM; Hong 2023 3D-LLM] makes this vision technically plausible. A VLM can interpret a photograph or architectural drawing, extract the spatial layout of a room including dimensions and major obstacles, and express this information in a structured format. If this structured output can be automatically converted into a CFD-ready mesh, the entire pathway from image to simulation result can be automated.

This paper presents and evaluates such a framework. Although the current implementation and benchmark focus on general indoor spaces rather than industrial facilities, the underlying methodology — VLM-based geometric abstraction from a single 2D image followed by automated CFD — is directly applicable to the industrial energy diagnostics scenario described above. The general indoor case is chosen as the starting point because it enables a controlled benchmark with well-defined complexity levels, while the extension to industrial environments remains a natural next step.

#### 1.2 Related work

**CFD for indoor environmental analysis.** CFD simulation of indoor environments encompasses ventilation effectiveness assessment [Kosutova 2019 cross-ventilation; Liu 2019 PV review], thermal comfort evaluation [Chen 2021 thermal comfort CFD], contaminant and pathogen dispersion modelling [Vuorinen 2020 aerosol; Liu 2021 restaurant COVID; Srivastava 2021 ventilation COVID], and building energy performance analysis [Brozovsky 2022 microclimate energy; Ren 2019 linear ventilation]. In industrial contexts, CFD has been applied to furnace energy efficiency [Zhao 2021 reheating furnace], process ventilation, and occupational exposure assessment, but its use remains far less systematic than in commercial building design due to the geometry preparation overhead.

**Geometry acquisition and the automation gap.** The geometry preparation bottleneck has motivated several automation strategies. BIM platforms can export geometry to CFD tools [Utkucu 2020 BIM interoperability; Hou 2022 BIM CFD prediction], but BIM penetration in the industrial sector is limited [Gourlis 2017 industrial BIM energy]. Scan-to-BIM methods [Rocha 2020 scan-to-BIM; Wang 2019 scan-to-BIM] produce detailed as-built models from point clouds, but the equipment cost and post-processing requirements are prohibitive for routine energy audits. Image-based 3D reconstruction methods, including neural radiance fields [Mildenhall 2020 NeRF], produce visually impressive results but yield representations (implicit volumetric fields, noisy point clouds) that cannot be directly used by finite-volume solvers. A fundamental mismatch exists between the output of reconstruction methods and the input requirements of CFD solvers.

**Data-driven flow prediction.** To bypass geometry preparation entirely, data-driven approaches predict flow fields from images or geometric parameters using neural networks [Guo 2016 CNN flow prediction; Calzolari 2021 DL replace CFD; Kastner 2023 GAN urban wind]. These methods achieve impressive speed but are limited to their training domain and cannot provide the conservation guarantees of physics-based solvers. For engineering applications where liability and physical correctness matter, retaining the full CFD solver is preferable.

**LLM-assisted CFD workflows.** Recent work has begun to explore large language models as assistants for CFD simulation workflows. FLUID-GPT [Yang 2023 FLUID-GPT] used a generative pre-trained transformer as a surrogate to predict erosion patterns, replacing brute-force CFD calculations with learned mappings. OpenFOAMGPT [Pandey 2025 OpenFOAMGPT] deployed a retrieval-augmented LLM agent to configure and execute OpenFOAM simulations through natural language interaction. Dong et al. [Dong 2025 LLM CFD] fine-tuned a domain-specific LLM to automate CFD case setup from textual problem descriptions. These studies demonstrate the growing integration of language models into CFD practice, but they address the *downstream* workflow — solver configuration, parameter selection, and result interpretation — while assuming that the geometric model already exists. The *upstream* bottleneck of geometry extraction from visual input, which the present work addresses, remains untouched by these approaches.

**Vision-language models for spatial understanding.** VLMs such as GPT-4 [OpenAI 2023 GPT-4] and Gemini [Google 2024 Gemini] have demonstrated spatial reasoning capabilities including object localisation, dimension estimation, and scene layout interpretation [Chen 2024 SpatialVLM; Hong 2023 3D-LLM]. These capabilities align with the information requirements of coarse indoor geometry specification: room dimensions, obstacle placement, and opening identification. The present work leverages this alignment by using a VLM not to reconstruct the scene geometry in full fidelity, but to abstract it into a simulation-compatible schema — a fundamentally different task that prioritises topological correctness and approximate dimensional accuracy over surface-level detail.

#### 1.3 Contributions

This paper contributes the following:

1. An automated image-to-CFD framework that eliminates the manual geometry preparation step by using a VLM to extract a simulation-ready 3D scene description from a single 2D image, targeting screening-level indoor airflow analysis.
2. A systematic benchmark comprising 100 evaluation cases (20 geometries × 5 view types) with independently computed reference CFD solutions, providing the first quantitative assessment of VLM-based geometric abstraction for downstream CFD accuracy.
3. An evaluation methodology that decouples geometric fidelity from flow-field accuracy, revealing that structural similarity does not guarantee CFD agreement — a finding with practical implications for acceptable error thresholds in screening applications.
4. Identification of three characteristic failure modes (composite-room collapse, obstacle hallucination, structure-versus-fidelity gap) and analysis of their practical impact, informing the design of future VLM-based simulation tools.
