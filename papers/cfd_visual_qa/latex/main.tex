\title{Can Vision-Language Models Evaluate CFD? A Benchmark for Physical Plausibility Assessment of Flow Field Visualizations}

\author[1]{Younghwan Joo\corref{cor1}}
\ead{yhjoo@kier.re.kr}

\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}
\address[1]{Korea Institute of Energy Research (KIER), Daejeon, Republic of Korea}

\begin{abstract}
Validating computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation results remains a manual bottleneck in automated pipelines, requiring expert visual inspection of flow field images. We introduce CFD-VisQA, the first benchmark for evaluating vision-language model (VLM) capabilities in assessing physical plausibility of CFD visualizations. The benchmark comprises 10 canonical thermal-fluid scenarios with 60 OpenFOAM cases, 258 flow field images, and 279 physics-grounded questions at 5 difficulty levels. Using an API-isolated, blind evaluation protocol with base64-encoded images and three independent trials per model, we evaluate Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and a domain expert on 30 common items under two conditions: setup-conditioned (with problem description) and image-only. The results reveal a striking rank reversal: with setup text, Claude leads at 88.9\% (zero false negatives across all trials) followed by GPT-5.4 at 80.0\% and the expert at 73.8\%; without setup text, the expert leads at 66.7\% while Claude drops to 33.3\%, the lowest among all evaluators. Claude's accuracy drop of 55.6 percentage points---the largest observed---confirms that its strength derives entirely from systematic cross-referencing of stated parameters against visual features, a capability that collapses without textual context. The expert's stability ($-$7.1\,pp) reflects a gestalt assessment strategy that derives limited benefit from setup parameters. We further document that subagent-based evaluation inflates VLM accuracy from 88.9\% to 99.6\% due to potential filesystem contamination, establishing API isolation as a methodological requirement for trustworthy VLM benchmarking. The benchmark dataset and evaluation pipeline are publicly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is increasingly integrated into automated design and analysis pipelines, from building ventilation assessment to industrial heat exchanger optimization. Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) have enabled new paradigms such as image-to-CFD frameworks, where a VLM extracts geometric information from architectural images to automatically generate CFD simulation domains. As these automated pipelines proliferate, a critical bottleneck emerges: \textit{who validates the results?}

In traditional CFD workflows, an experienced engineer inspects velocity contours, temperature fields, and streamline plots to confirm that the solution is physically reasonable, checking for proper boundary layer development, expected recirculation patterns, and absence of numerical artifacts. This visual inspection requires domain expertise accumulated over years and cannot scale with the throughput of automated pipelines. If a VLM could perform this validation step---assessing whether a CFD flow field visualization is physically plausible---the automation loop would close entirely.

Yet it remains unknown whether current VLMs possess the physical reasoning capability to evaluate CFD output images. Existing VLM benchmarks test scientific knowledge through multiple-choice questions on textbook diagrams \cite{yue2024mmmu}, chart comprehension \cite{pandey2025}, or everyday physics understanding \cite{chow2025physbench}, but none evaluate the ability to assess simulation output images against known physical laws. The closest work evaluates VLMs on meteorological heatmaps \cite{chen2024climateIQA} or tests generative AI on fluid mechanics prompts \cite{kashefi2024}, but neither addresses the specific task of CFD result validation.

This paper introduces \textbf{CFD-VisQA}, the first benchmark designed to evaluate VLM capabilities in assessing physical plausibility of CFD flow field visualizations. The benchmark comprises 10 canonical thermal-fluid scenarios (natural convection, forced convection, mixed convection), each with correct solutions and systematically generated erroneous solutions spanning 6 error types. We evaluate three frontier VLMs (Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5.4, and Gemini 3.1 Pro) against a domain expert using an API-isolated, multi-trial protocol on 30 evaluation items under two information conditions, revealing a striking rank reversal: VLMs that excel with setup text perform worst without it.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{CFD-VisQA benchmark dataset}: 60 OpenFOAM CFD cases across 10 thermal-fluid scenarios, producing 258 flow field images with 279 physics-grounded questions and expert annotations, with both correct solutions and 6 types of intentionally generated errors.

\item \textbf{API-isolated evaluation protocol}: We demonstrate that subagent-based VLM evaluation inflates accuracy (99.6\% vs.\ 88.9\%) due to potential filesystem contamination, and establish base64-encoded API calls as a methodological requirement for trustworthy benchmarking.

\item \textbf{Rank reversal between evaluation conditions}: With setup text, VLMs outperform the expert (Claude 88.9\%, GPT-5.4 80.0\% vs.\ Expert 73.8\%); without setup text, the ranking inverts (Expert 66.7\% vs.\ Claude 33.3\%). This reveals that VLM performance derives from setup--image cross-referencing, not visual understanding of physics.

\item \textbf{Model-specific setup dependency}: Claude drops 55.6\,pp without setup text, GPT-5.4 drops 36.7\,pp, while the expert drops only 7.1\,pp. The magnitude of setup dependency is itself a diagnostic of each evaluator's assessment strategy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{VLM Benchmarks for Scientific Visuals}

Several benchmarks evaluate VLM performance on scientific and technical imagery, though none address CFD specifically. MMMU \cite{yue2024mmmu} tests college-level reasoning across 30 disciplines with 11,500 questions, including engineering diagrams, but contains no flow field images. SciFIBench \cite{roberts2024} evaluates 28 large multimodal models on 2,000 scientific figure interpretation tasks from arXiv papers, finding that VLM capabilities in scientific figure understanding are ``not well characterised.'' PhysBench \cite{chow2025physbench} tests 75 VLMs on physical world understanding across 10,002 entries, finding that the best model (GPT-4o) achieves only 49.5\% vs.\ 95.9\% human accuracy; however, its ``fluid dynamics'' category covers everyday scenarios (water pouring) rather than simulation outputs.

Most relevant to our work is ClimateIQA \cite{chen2024climateIQA}, which constructs 26,280 meteorological heatmap images with 762,120 instruction samples for VLM evaluation. Like CFD contour plots, meteorological heatmaps require colormap interpretation and spatial pattern recognition. We extend this line of work from meteorological to engineering simulation imagery, with the additional challenge of physics-grounded plausibility assessment.

\subsection{AI for CFD Result Assessment}

The intersection of AI and CFD has been explored primarily through surrogate modeling and simulation automation, not result validation. CFDLLMBench \cite{somasekharan2025} evaluates LLMs on CFD knowledge (92\% on conceptual MCQs) and OpenFOAM case generation (25--34\% success), but includes no visual component. The only published work directly evaluating VLMs on CFD visualizations is a 2026 study on mixed-reality indoor CFD images \cite{mdpi2026vlmcfd}, which fine-tunes Qwen2.5-VL from $<$30\% to $>$60\% accuracy on domain-specific questions. Kashefi \cite{kashefi2024} demonstrates that generative AI systems produce physically incorrect fluid mechanics imagery, motivating the need for a public CFD visualization benchmark.

\subsection{Research Gap}

No existing benchmark evaluates VLM capability to assess physical plausibility of CFD simulation output images. CFD-VisQA fills this gap with a dedicated benchmark combining physics-grounded questions, systematically generated errors, and an API-isolated evaluation protocol that addresses contamination risks inherent in agent-based evaluation.

\section{Benchmark Design}

\subsection{Design Philosophy}

The CFD-VisQA benchmark is built on three principles. First, it must measure a spectrum of visual understanding capabilities, from basic colorbar reading to physics-grounded plausibility assessment. Second, it requires both correct and intentionally erroneous flow fields. Third, all data must be reproducible: every flow field is generated from OpenFOAM simulations with published scripts. The benchmark is organized along three orthogonal axes (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark-overview}): flow scenarios, visualization types, and question difficulty levels.

\subsection{Error Taxonomy}

We define six error types, each produced by a controlled perturbation (Fig.~\ref{fig:error-examples}): E1 under-convergence (30 iterations), E2 boundary condition swap, E3 wrong viscosity ($10$--$100\times$), E4 wrong turbulence model (laminar at turbulent Re), E5 coarse mesh (${\sim}10\times$ reduced), and E8 gravity/direction reversal.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

We implement an API-isolated, blind evaluation protocol (Fig.~\ref{fig:eval-protocol}). Each flow field image is base64-encoded and sent directly to VLM APIs (Anthropic, Google GenAI, OpenAI) alongside optional problem setup text. The model receives only the image bytes and prompt text---no filesystem access, no project context, no shared state between items. Each of the 30 evaluation items is an independent API call. Three independent trials are conducted per model to assess consistency, using default API temperature settings.

This protocol was adopted after discovering that subagent-based evaluation (where a VLM runs within an agent framework with filesystem access) inflated Claude's accuracy from 88.9\% to 99.6\%, likely due to the agent's ability to access ground truth files in the project directory (Section~5.4).

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\caption{API-isolated evaluation protocol. Flow fields are rendered, base64-encoded, and sent via direct API calls with optional setup text. An isolation barrier ensures VLMs have zero filesystem access. Three independent trials per model assess consistency.}
\label{fig:eval-protocol}
\end{figure}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Overall Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:main-results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:main-accuracy}a summarize setup-conditioned accuracy across three independent trials. Claude Opus 4.6 achieves the highest mean accuracy at 88.9\% with zero false negatives across all trials---every error case is detected, though 3--4 correct cases are over-flagged per trial. GPT-5.4 follows at 80.0\% with remarkable trial consistency (identical across all three trials). The domain expert achieves 73.8\%. Gemini 3.1 Pro scores lowest at 57.8\% with the highest variance across trials (53.3\%--63.3\%).

\subsection{Contamination and Protocol Integrity}

During protocol development, we discovered that the evaluation method itself materially affects reported accuracy. Fig.~\ref{fig:trial-consistency}b compares Claude's setup-conditioned accuracy across three isolation levels:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Subagent} (99.6\%): The VLM runs as a subagent within an agent framework, with filesystem access to the project directory containing ground truth labels.
\item \textbf{CLI-isolated} (83.3\%): The VLM runs via a separate CLI process with restricted tools but shared filesystem.
\item \textbf{API-isolated} (88.9\%): The VLM receives only base64-encoded images via direct API calls, with zero filesystem access.
\end{itemize}

The 10.7\,pp inflation from API-isolated to subagent evaluation is not a ``contamination paradox'' but a contamination \textit{threat}: the subagent's filesystem access likely allowed it to read ground truth files, artificially boosting accuracy. This finding establishes API isolation as a methodological requirement for trustworthy VLM benchmarking.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Setup Text as the Critical Variable}

The rank reversal (Fig.~\ref{fig:rank-reversal}) is the central finding of this work. It demonstrates that VLMs do not ``understand'' CFD flow physics in the way a domain expert does. Instead, the strongest VLMs (Claude, GPT-5.4) perform a form of systematic cross-referencing: comparing stated parameters (Re, Ra, boundary temperatures, lid direction) against observable image features (development length, colorbar values, vortex rotation). When this textual anchor is removed, their performance collapses below that of the expert.

The expert, by contrast, employs a gestalt assessment strategy: evaluating whether the overall flow pattern ``looks right'' based on years of experience. This strategy is robust to the absence of setup text ($-$7.1\,pp) because it relies on internalized physical intuition rather than explicit parameter checking. However, it is vulnerable to ``subtle'' errors where the flow is qualitatively correct---boundary condition swaps that produce mirror images (20\% recall), wrong viscosity that produces smoother-than-expected flows (29\% recall).

The expert reads the setup text but cannot readily translate dimensionless parameters into quantitative visual expectations. An expert knows that higher Re produces flatter velocity profiles, but does not mentally compute the hydrodynamic entry length ($L_e \approx 0.05\,\mathrm{Re}\,D$) and check it against the image scale. The evaluation remains qualitative. Claude, by contrast, appears to perform this quantitative cross-referencing explicitly.

\subsection{Model-Specific Capabilities}

The three VLMs exhibit fundamentally different profiles:

\textbf{Claude Opus 4.6} is the strongest cross-referencer: FN = 0 with setup text (catches every error) but worst without it (33.3\%). Its 55.6\,pp drop is diagnostic of near-total reliance on setup--image comparison.

\textbf{GPT-5.4} shows moderate cross-referencing (80.0\% setup, $-$36.7\,pp drop) with remarkable consistency (identical accuracy across all three setup-conditioned trials). It misses some wrong-viscosity and underconvergence errors that Claude catches.

\textbf{Gemini 3.1 Pro} shows the weakest setup dependency ($-$4.4\,pp) and highest variance across trials. Its setup-conditioned accuracy (57.8\%) is lower than the expert's (73.8\%), suggesting limited cross-referencing capability. Its image-only performance (53.3\%) is second only to the expert.

\subsection{Implications for CFD Automation}

These results have practical implications for deploying VLMs as automated CFD validators:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Setup text is mandatory}: Without problem parameters, no VLM matches the expert. Setup-conditioned prompting is not optional but essential.
\item \textbf{Model selection matters}: Only Claude achieves FN = 0 (catches all errors). GPT-5.4 is a viable alternative at lower cost with good consistency.
\item \textbf{False positives are the trade-off}: Claude's FN = 0 comes with FP = 3--4 per trial. A practical pipeline should flag these for expert review rather than auto-reject.
\item \textbf{API isolation is a protocol requirement}: Subagent-based evaluation inflates accuracy by $\sim$10\,pp. Published VLM benchmarks should specify the isolation level of their evaluation method.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

Sample size is limited to 30 items for VLM evaluation and 80 for the expert. A single domain expert serves as the human baseline; multi-expert evaluation would strengthen the comparison. All scenarios are 2D steady-state RANS simulations; 3D visualizations, transient flows, and multi-physics problems present additional challenges. VLM evaluations used default API temperature settings; deterministic decoding (temperature = 0) might reduce trial-to-trial variance for Gemini. The benchmark should be extended to open-source VLM architectures to broaden generalizability.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced CFD-VisQA, the first benchmark for evaluating VLM capabilities in CFD flow field plausibility assessment. API-isolated evaluation of three frontier VLMs reveals that \textbf{setup text is the critical variable}: with problem parameters, Claude Opus 4.6 achieves 88.9\% accuracy with zero false negatives, outperforming both GPT-5.4 (80.0\%) and the domain expert (73.8\%); without setup text, the ranking completely inverts, with the expert leading at 66.7\% and Claude falling to 33.3\%.

This rank reversal demonstrates that VLM performance on CFD validation derives from setup--image cross-referencing rather than visual understanding of physics. The expert's stability without setup text reflects gestalt assessment grounded in physical intuition---a capability no current VLM replicates. We further establish that evaluation protocol design materially affects results: subagent-based evaluation inflated Claude's accuracy by over 10 percentage points due to filesystem contamination, making API isolation a methodological requirement.

For practical deployment, the results support using Claude Opus with setup-conditioned prompts as a first-pass automated validator (FN = 0), with expert review of flagged cases to resolve false positives. The benchmark dataset and evaluation pipeline are publicly available to support further research.
