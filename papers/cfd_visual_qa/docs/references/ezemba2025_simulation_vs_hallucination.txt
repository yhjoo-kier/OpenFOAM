ABSTRACT
Engineering simulations generate complex multimodal data that
are crucial for design iteration and validation. The interpretation of
these simulations traditionally requires significant domain exper-
tise and cognitive effort. Recently, vision-language models (VLMs)
have demonstrated impressive capabilities in general-domain multi-
modal reasoning tasks, offering the potential for automating simula-
tion data interpretation. However, the effectiveness of these models
in specialized engineering contexts remains largely unexplored.
This paper presents an initial comparative evaluation of state-of-
the-art VLMs on question answering tasks involving structural
and fluid dynamics simulation data across three modalities: text,
images, and videos. In doing so, we introduce a domain-specific
benchmark dataset comprising true/false questions testing com-
prehension for engineering simulations. Unlike general-purpose
multimodal benchmarks, our evaluation focuses specifically on the
technical interpretation of engineering simulation outputs, requir-
ing specialized domain knowledge and physical reasoning that is
absent from broader multimedia assessments. Our results demon-
strate that text modality yields substantially higher performance
(up to 69.2% accuracy with GPT-4o, 66.3% with LLaVA) than visual
inputs (52.9-55.8%), with GPT-4o, LLaVA, and Phi-3 exhibiting the
strongest capabilities for text comprehension. Models on average
performed better on structural analysis tasks than fluid dynamics
problems, with minimal advantage observed for native video pro-
cessing over batched image approaches. However, reliability is still
far below that needed for engineering applications, highlighting sig-
nificant challenges in applying current VLMs to the interpretation
of engineering simulations.

INTRODUCTION
Engineering simulations have become essential tools for under-
standing complex phenomena in structural mechanics and fluid
dynamics. These simulations help engineers predict system behav-
ior, optimize designs, and ensure safety across applications ranging
from civil infrastructure to aerospace components [4, 11, 13]. With
the increasing complexity and volume of these simulations, there is
a growing need for automated systems that can interpret and rea-
son about simulation results through natural language interactions,
regardless of their original format [10]. Vision language models
(VLM) have demonstrated remarkable abilities in understanding
and reasoning about visual information paired with text in general
domains [18]. Models such as BLIP[16], Llama[19], Qwen[23], and
GPT-4o[20] have achieved impressive performance on benchmarks
for image captioning, visual question-answering, and multimodal
reasoning tasks. These models leverage transformer architectures
to create aligned representations between visual and textual in-
formation, enabling rich cross-modal understanding. These multi-
modal performances are typically validated using benchmarks like
MM-Vet 2[25], ReForm-Eval[17], and ScienceQA[22] for general
reasoning capabilities.
However, the application of these powerful VLMs to specialized
engineering domains, particularly simulation data interpretation,
remains relatively underexplored. Firstly, existing benchmarks do
not address the specialized requirements of engineering simulation
interpretation. These general benchmarks focus on everyday un-
derstanding rather than the technical visualization conventions,
physical principles, and domain-specific reasoning required for
engineering simulation analysis. Secondly, engineering simula-
tions present unique data challenges that common VLMs don’t
account for. Engineering simulations generate vast amounts of
data in specialized engineering formats. To leverage modern off-
the-shelf vision-language models, this diverse engineering data is
converted to VLM-compatible data like images, videos, and text
outputs. However, even in a VLM compatible format, simulation
data still presents unique challenges, including the need to un-
derstand spatial relationships, identify critical structural patterns,
interpret color-coded flow fields, and reason about physical phe-
nomena. While researchers have made progress in related engineer-
ing applications—such as generating multimodal VLM-compatible
engineering data for aerodynamic car designs [7], evaluating LLMs’

understanding of engineering documentation [2, 6], generating
multimodal engineering material data[14], and exploring vision-

have primarily focused on design generation and documentation

to the best of the authors’ knowledge, current research lacks a sys-

multimodal simulation data that include images, videos, and text
output across both structural and fluid dynamics domains.
In this paper, we present a comparative analysis of several state-
of-the-art vision-language models for question-answering tasks on
fluid dynamics and structural simulation data. For each simulation,
we examine four input modalities (text, images, videos represented
as frames, and native video), using leading models for each. The
contributions of this paper are (1) the introduction of a benchmark
dataset of engineering simulations with expert-crafted questions,
(2) a cross-modal evaluation methodology to compare model per-
formance across images, videos, and text, (3), the identification of
key limitations in current vision-language models for interpreting
technical data, offering insights for future model development and
engineering applications.
2
SIMULATION DATASET CREATION
This section describes our approach to creating a benchmark dataset
for evaluating vision-language models on engineering simulation
data interpretation tasks.
2.1
Evaluation Approach
To systematically evaluate VLM performance on simulation data,
we developed a binary question-answering framework inspired by
the StrategyQA benchmark [8]. For each simulation type, either
fluid or structural, we created a set of true/false questions requiring
an understanding of fundamental engineering concepts to correctly
interpret the simulation. This approach is advantageous because
binary questions provide objective evaluation metrics. Questions
can be designed to test specific physical concepts, and results can
easily be aggregated through standard accuracy metrics.
2.2
Simulation Data Sources
All simulation data was sourced from publicly available Ansys ex-
amples to ensure simulations with proper boundary conditions,
domain diversity across various engineering applications, consis-
tency in visualization formats and data representation, and poten-
tial in-distribution evaluation, as models may have encountered
similar examples during pretraining. We selected twelve distinct
simulation examples (six for static structural analysis and six for
fluid dynamics) that collectively covered a broad range of physical
phenomena and geometric configurations, as shown in Figure 1.
2.2.1
Static Structural Simulation Data. Static structural simula-
tions focus on the analysis of component deformation and stress
under varied loading conditions [12]. Key outputs include von Mises
stress distributions, which combine a complex stress tensor into
a scalar value for failure prediction [5], and deformation patterns
showing how components elastically change shape in response
to applied loads. Each structural simulation in this work was con-
figured using Ansys Mechanical with standard settings for mesh
quality, solution convergence criteria, and material properties. The
simulations represent steady-state solutions under static loading
conditions.
2.2.2
Fluid Dynamics Simulation Data. Fluid dynamics simulations
focus on fluid flow patterns, pressure distributions, and velocity
fields[24]. Key outputs include pressure distribution maps show-
ing regions of high and low pressure, velocity vector fields indi-
cating flow direction and magnitude, and streamlines visualizing
flow paths through the domain. Each fluid simulation in this work
was configured using Ansys Fluent with appropriate boundary
conditions and convergence criteria. The simulations represent
steady-state flow conditions.
2.3
Multimodal Data Extraction
From each simulation, we extracted three distinct modalities of
data using PyMechanical and PyFluent, Ansys’s Python scripting
interfaces.
2.3.1
Image Data. Static images were captured from simulation
outputs in a standardized planar view. For structural simulations,
images displayed von Mises stress distributions with color legends,
numerical scales, orientation axes, and deformation information.
For fluid simulations, images showed pressure distributions with
corresponding scales and reference axes. All images included consis-
tent color mapping schemes and numerical annotations to provide
quantitative context, as shown in Figure 1.
2.3.2
Video Data. Videos were generated as animations, showing
the temporal progression of the simulation towards steady state. For
structural simulations, videos depicted the progressive application
of loads and the resulting deformation. For fluid simulations, videos
showed fluid movement and pressure changes over time. These
animations were not new transient analyses but simple animated
visualizations of the steady-state solutions to enhance understand-
ing of the physical behavior.
2.3.3
Text Data. Two levels of textual data were extracted. First,
comprehensive data files were extracted, containing detailed simu-
lation parameters, mesh information, boundary conditions, and nu-
merical results. These files were substantial (approximately 800,000
tokens for structural and 20,000,000 tokens for fluid), so we pro-
duced condensed summary reports generated with Ansys report
tools containing key simulation parameters, material properties,
boundary conditions, and scalar results, providing a higher-level
overview of the simulation setup and outcome.
2.4
Question-Answer Pair Generation
We developed 20 questions for each type of simulation, designed
to test the understanding of fundamental engineering concepts.
Questions were formulated by engineering domain experts to (1)
ensure they addressed core physical principles relevant to each sim-
ulation type, (2) required the interpretation of visual or textual data
to answer correctly, (3) covered diverse aspects of simulation under-
standing, and (4) were geometry-agnostic to evaluate generalizable
reasoning across problems. The full list of these questions is pro-
vided in Table 3. The dataset includes specific validation questions

material properties, and modeling assumptions. These questions
reflect the critical evaluation an engineer would perform to assess
simulation validity and applicability. Third, verification questions
were developed to detect guess-like behavior and ensure consistent
physical understanding. This approach to question development en-
sured comprehensive coverage of both domains while maintaining
consistency in evaluation metrics across all test cases and modali-
ties.

3.2
Selected Vision-Language Models
Table 2 summarizes our selection of vision-language models across
the three modalities: image, video, and text. For image processing,
we employed five state-of-the-art models representing different
architectural approaches: Meta’s Llama-3.2-11B-Vision, Alibaba’s
Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, Salesforce’s BLIP2-opt-2.7b, Microsoft’s
Kosmos-2, and OpenAI’s GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-08-06). Our video
processing evaluation utilized two distinct approaches: models with
native video processing capabilities (Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct and
LLaVA-onevision) processed videos directly through specialized
modules, while models without native support (BLIP2, Kosmos-2,
and GPT-4o) employed a frame-sampling strategy where sequences
of extracted frames were processed as batched images. Notably,
we excluded Llama-3.2-11B-Vision from video tasks based on doc-
umented performance degradation with sequential images2. For
text-only processing of simulation reports, we evaluated five models
(Llama-3.2, Qwen2.5, Phi-3, LLaVA-onevision, and GPT-4o) while
excluding BLIP2 and Kosmos-2 after preliminary tests revealed
their suboptimal performance on text-only tasks.

Implementation Details
3.3.1
Image Processing Implementation. All image processing mod-
els were run with full floating-point precision to maintain accuracy.
For Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct, we adjusted the resolution parameters
to fit memory constraints, setting minimum pixels to 256×28×28

3.3.2
Video Processing Implementation. For native video process-
ing models, we configured specific hyperparameters based on the
model requirements. LLaVA-Onevision and Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct
were configured to process 8 frames per video. For models without
native video processing (BLIP2-Opt, Kosmos-2, and GPT-4o), we
implemented a frame-sampling approach, extracting one frame
every 10 seconds from each video.
This specific sampling rate was determined through systematic
experimentation to optimize the trade-off between temporal infor-
mation capture and processing efficiency. The 10-second interval
was selected to ensure each sampled frame contained meaningful
unique information while maintaining consistency across all sim-
ulations. The original videos were generated at a fixed frame rate
using PyMechanical and PyFluent, and our sampling approach
ensured we captured key state changes in the simulations without
excessive redundancy. This approach aligns with current practices
in vision-language modeling, where deduplication of video frames
often improves model performance by focusing on distinct visual
states rather than redundant temporal information [26]. The sam-
pling approach was kept consistent across all simulations to ensure
fair comparison. For shorter videos, this resulted in fewer total
frames, while longer videos yielded proportionally more frames.
We maintained this consistent sampling rate rather than using a
fixed number of frames per video to preserve temporal resolution
across simulations of varying duration. This approach ensured that
transient phenomena and state changes were captured at similar
temporal granularity regardless of video length. Our uniform sam-
pling approach ensured comparability across all models and simu-
lation types while effectively capturing the key temporal dynamics
in each simulation.
3.3.3
Text Processing Implementation. Processing the extensive
simulation text files (Structural: ≈800, 000 tokens; Fluid: ≈20, 000, 000
tokens) demanded a method to manage the large text context. To
address this, we used Ansys report tool to extract relevant simula-
tion parameters, effectively reducing text input and enabling direct
processing by the models. We focused on models with sufficiently
large context windows, such as Llama, Phi-3, and GPT-4o (up to
128,000 tokens), and excluded those with limited capacities, such as
BLIP. All text processing models used bfloat16 precision to ensure
fair comparison.

RESULTS
Our evaluation revealed a clear performance hierarchy across modal-
ities. Text modality consistently produced the highest accuracy
scores across all models, with GPT-4o (69.2%), LLaVA (66.3%), and
Phi-3 (62.5%) demonstrating particularly strong text comprehension
capabilities. Visual modalities yielded significantly lower perfor-
mance, with most models achieving 53-56% accuracy regardless of
whether processing images or videos. Among image-processing
models, GPT-4o showed a marginal advantage (55.8%), followed
by Llama (54.6%); GPT-4o and LLaVA led in video interpretation
(55.8% and 53.8%, respectively). These results suggest that current

4.1
Performance on Fluid Dynamics Tasks
Vision-language models demonstrated moderate performance on
fluid dynamics tasks, with accuracy ranging from 50% to 60% in dif-
ferent modalities and models (Figure 2). This performance suggests
a limited understanding of complex fluid phenomena represented
in the simulation outputs. LLaVA achieved the highest accuracy
on the fluid dynamics text data (60%), followed by GPT-4o (58.3%).
For video data, LLaVA and GPT-4o led with similar accuracy (55%
and 54.2% respectively), while other models reached 50%. For the
image modality, GPT-4o showed slightly better performance (54.2%)
compared to other models’ consistent 50% accuracy.
Analysis of specific test cases reveals significant performance
variability. All models showed consistently high performance on
the falling-sphere viscometer test case (70-75%), suggesting that
simple laminar flow patterns may be more readily interpretable. In

contrast, the Natural Convection test case proved challenging for
all models (around 40%), indicating difficulty in understanding com-

In particular, while models showed better performance on some

The minimal difference between native video processing and

GPT-4o at 54.2%) indicates that current models derive limited benefit
from temporal information in fluid simulation videos, despite the
dynamic nature of fluid phenomena.
4.2
Performance on Structural Analysis Tasks
Vision-language models on average demonstrated better perfor-
mance on structural analysis tasks compared to fluid dynamics, with
accuracy ranging from 49% to 80% across modalities and models
(Figure 3). This suggests greater ability to interpret stress distri-
butions and deformation patterns compared to fluid simulations.

marginal decrease to 52.5% compared to the 55.8% achieved by the
others. For the image modality, Llama demonstrated a marginally
better performance at 59.2% accuracy, followed closely by GPT-4o
at 57.5% while the remaining models displayed comparable results
at 55.8%

5
DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Current VLMs fall short of the reliability needed for autonomous
engineering analysis. In these domains, the results suggest that
models possess stronger language understanding capabilities than
specialized visual interpretation skills for technical domains. This
is perhaps unsurprising given the intent and modality for which
these models were originally developed. The superior performance
on text modality (up to 80% for GPT-4o) indicates that engineering
summary reports may currently be the most reliable input format
for VLM-based simulation interpretation. The low performance on
visual modalities is particularly concerning, as these accuracy levels
are only marginally better than random guessing (50%) (see Table
4 in the Appendix for more information). This indicates no mean-
ingful comprehension of visual engineering data. Most concerning
is the models’ failure on fundamental validation questions that
should have consistent answers across all simulations—such as "Do
reaction forces balance external loads?" for structural simulations
and "Is mass conservation satisfied?" for fluid simulations (Figure
4).
Analysis of paired opposing questions revealed frequent incon-
sistencies, where models would simultaneously affirm mutually ex-
clusive conditions and disagree on paired similar questions. These
patterns indicate guess-like behavior rather than structured rea-
soning based on physical understanding. A detailed analysis of
individual paired questions is presented in Figures 5 and 6 of the
Appendix. The consistent performance gap between the structural
and fluid domains highlights the varying difficulty in interpreting
different physical simulations. Structural simulations, with their
well-defined stress patterns and color mappings, appear more ac-
cessible to current models than fluid simulations with complex flow
patterns and pressure distributions. This domain-specific perfor-
mance variance should inform expectations when applying VLMs
to different engineering disciplines.
Several limitations in current VLM capabilities for engineering
applications emerge from our analysis:
• Limited physical reasoning: While models show knowledge
of basic principles, they struggle to apply physical reasoning
to visual interpretation tasks.
• Challenges with specialized visualizations: Technical con-
ventions in engineering visualizations appear less accessible
to models than general domain visual content.
• Architectural limitations: The minimal advantage of native
video processing over batched image approaches suggests
inadequate temporal processing mechanisms.
• Logical inconsistency: Models frequently provide contradic-
tory answers to paired questions testing the same physical
concept.
Future work could explore several approaches to address the
limitations identified in our study. Researchers might investigate
domain-specific pretraining using paired simulation outputs and
expert annotations to familiarize models with engineering visu-
alizations. Fine-tuning strategies with engineering examples or
developing specialized prompt engineering techniques could poten-
tially improve performance without extensive model modifications.
Architectural enhancements to multimodal attention mechanisms
might better align visual features with engineering domain knowl-
edge. Expanded benchmark datasets specifically for engineering
simulations would also facilitate progress in this specialized domain.
These directions represent promising avenues for improving the
reliability of vision-language models in engineering simulation in-
terpretation tasks. Additionally, future iterations of this benchmark
could benefit from multiple-choice question formats that provide
more nuanced evaluation while maintaining the ability to detect
reasoning inconsistencies. In addition, our results suggest that spe-
cialized architectural features may be more important than raw
parameter count for engineering domain tasks.
Ultimately, the ability of the community to address goals in de-
sign automation is only as good as our ability to measure the capabil-
ities of novel algorithms. Therefore, it is of paramount importance
for future research to establish more extensive and comprehensive
benchmarks for specific engineering disciplines to drive progress.

QUESTIONS USED FOR BENCHMARKING
Table 3 presents the complete set of benchmark questions used in
our evaluation framework. For structural analysis, questions cover
key aspects including stress analysis, material behavior, boundary
conditions, and convergence properties. For fluid analysis, ques-
tions address flow regimes, conservation principles, and special
flow phenomena. These questions were carefully designed to test
both fundamental understanding of engineering principles and the
ability to extract relevant information from simulation visualiza-
tions while being geometrically agnostic. Each question requires a
binary (True/False) response, allowing for objective evaluation of
model performance.

Figure 5 illustrates the average contradiction rates observed when

better logical consistency. Stress concentration vs. factor of safety
questions ("Are there any stress concentrations exceeding mate-

varied performance with GPT-4o (27.7%) and Qwen (22.3%) ex-
hibiting higher contradiction rates. These patterns reveal signif-
icant variation in models’ ability to maintain logical consistency
across related engineering concepts. For fluid-related contradic-

dictions (0%), while GPT-4o (11.3%), Qwen (5.7%), and Phi-3 (17%)
showed relatively strong performance. Llama exhibited the highest

BATCHED IMAGE PROCESSING FOR
LLAVA-ONEVISION AND QWEN
Figure 7 shows the comparison between running LLava-Onevision
and Qwen with native video inputs or multi-images. LLaVA-Onevision
demonstrates marginal improvements when using native video pro-
cessing compared to multi-image inputs, with performance gains
of 0.8 percentage points in both structural (51.7% to 52.5%) and fluid

leverage some temporal information present in video sequences.

indicating that its video processing approach may not significantly
utilize temporal dynamics and instead processes videos essentially
as collections of independent frames.
